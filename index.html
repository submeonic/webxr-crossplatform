Hydrogen Atom

Before exploring how semiconductor devices work, it helps to understand the quantum behavior of atoms. Hydrogen has the simplest atomic structure, making it a useful starting point for seeing how repeated electron measurements form three-dimensional probability distributions called orbitals.

Simulation 01

The 1s Orbital

At atomic scales, an electron does not follow a predictable planetary orbit. Each pale point in this visualization represents one possible position found by measuring the electron in hydrogen’s lowest-energy state many times.

Move the thin spherical region through the cloud to count how many measurements occur at each distance from the nucleus. The most likely distance is neither at the nucleus nor infinitely far away; it occurs near one Bohr radius.

Simulation 02

The 2s Orbital

When hydrogen absorbs energy, its electron can occupy the excited 2s state. The probability cloud expands outward and separates into an inner region and a much larger outer region.

Near two Bohr radii, the probability falls to zero. This empty spherical boundary is a radial node. Compare the green 2s curve with the yellow 1s curve on the shared graph, then move the shell through the node and both probability regions.

Simulation 03

The 2p Orbital

The 2p orbitals have the same principal energy level as 2s, but their probability is directional instead of spherical. A nodal plane passes through the nucleus and separates the two lobes where electron measurements are likely to appear.

The three 2p orbitals share the same shape and differ only in orientation along the x, y, and z axes. This section will use direct object manipulation to make that three-dimensional structure easier to inspect.
