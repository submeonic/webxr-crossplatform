Hydrogen Orbitals in 3D

This 3D/XR experience extends Chapter 1 of LearnQM’s Semiconductor Physics Visualization. Instead of treating an orbital as a flat picture, you can turn it, scan it by radius, and compare what you see with the graph. The goal is to use the extra spatial dimension to explain where an electron may be measured—and where it cannot be measured—in each state.

Interaction Tutorial

Read a Probability Cloud

Imagine preparing the same hydrogen atom many times and measuring the electron’s position once each time. Every point is one possible outcome from that repeated experiment; the points are not a path traveled by one electron. Dense regions mean that outcomes pack closely in space. The graph answers a different question: how many outcomes lie within a thin spherical shell at each distance from the nucleus? Move the shell to connect those two views of probability.

On desktop or touch, drag mostly sideways to rotate and mostly vertically to scan the shell; use the wheel or a two-finger pinch to zoom. In WebXR, pinch to begin, then move sideways to rotate or up and down to scan. The guide follows whichever direction is farther from your starting point. Open either palm when no higher-priority interaction is active, then poke a menu item with the opposite index finger. When you stop interacting, a slow rotation resumes so the full 3D form remains visible.

Simulation 01

The 1s Orbital

The 1s orbital is hydrogen’s lowest-energy state. Its pale yellow cloud is most densely packed near the nucleus and thins smoothly with distance. Rotate it from several directions: if no viewpoint reveals an edge, lobe, or preferred direction, the evidence supports spherical symmetry.

Now scan the shell outward. Although the cloud is densest at the center, a shell very near the center contains little space and therefore few outcomes. Larger shells include more space. Their competition with the thinning cloud produces a radial-probability peak near one Bohr radius, a0. Continue outward past the peak: the fading tail has no hard boundary, even when only a few sample points remain visible.

Simulation 02

The 2s Orbital

The excited 2s state remains spherical, but its outcomes spread through a much larger volume. A compact inner region and a broad outer region are separated by a radial node at 2a0: an entire spherical surface where the probability of measuring the electron is zero.

A flat image can make this empty region look like a ring. Rotate the cloud and watch the gap remain wrapped around the nucleus; that motion reveals it as a sphere. Then scan from the inner peak, through the zero at the node, and into the larger outer peak. Compare the green 2s graph with the faint yellow 1s reference to explain how excitation changes both the size and internal structure of the probability distribution.

Simulation 03

The 2p Orbitals

In ideal hydrogen, 2s and 2p belong to the same principal energy level, yet their spatial patterns differ. A 2p cloud points along one axis and contains two lobes. Between them is an angular node: a whole plane through the nucleus where the probability is zero.

Switch among 2px, 2py, and 2pz with the viewer buttons or XR palm menu. The subscript names the axis along which the lobes point, so the nodal plane is perpendicular to that axis. Rotate the cloud and labeled axes together to inspect this relationship from another angle. Then scan the shell and switch orientations again: the 3D direction changes, but the pink radial graph does not. This shows that orientation changes angular probability while leaving the distribution of distances unchanged.
