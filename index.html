Hydrogen Orbitals in 3D

This 3D/XR experience extends Chapter 1 of LearnQM’s Semiconductor Physics Visualization. Instead of treating an orbital as a flat picture, you can turn it, scan distances in s orbitals or grow probability enclosures in p and d, and connect what you see with quantitative graphs. The goal is to use the extra spatial dimension to explain where an electron may be measured—and where it cannot be measured—in each state.

Interaction Tutorial

Read a Probability Cloud

Imagine preparing the same hydrogen atom many times and measuring the electron’s position once each time. Every point is one possible outcome from that repeated experiment; the points are not a path traveled by one electron. Dense regions mean that outcomes pack closely in space. In s orbitals, a thin moving shell connects distance to radial probability. In p and d orbitals, a shape-following enclosure connects the space it occupies to the probability it contains. Neither enclosure is a hard boundary of the orbital: probability extends beyond it.

Drag sideways to rotate. Drag vertically to scan the s shell or adjust p/d coverage from Off to 5–90%. Drag horizontally on a graph to select the radius or probability directly; arrow keys work when it is focused. Wheel or two-finger pinch zooms. In WebXR, pinch and move sideways to rotate or vertically to adjust the same measurement. Open either palm, choose an s, p, or d tab, then poke a choice with the opposite index finger. Each new family starts with its first orbital. Resample and Exit XR stay available; only s orbitals have a 90% Region switch.

Every newly selected orbital reveals its outcomes over about three seconds, slowly at first and then faster. Resample draws a fresh set. This timing illustrates repeated measurements, not an electron moving or an experimental measurement rate. Highlighted dots are inside the current shell or enclosure. For p/d, the colored graph is the model; yellow dots are the fraction of plotted outcomes actually enclosed. Expect fluctuations, especially while only a few outcomes are visible. The model and enclosure stay fixed when you resample. Volume is measured in cubic Bohr radii, a0³; the model curve begins at 5%.

Simulation 01

The 1s Orbital

The 1s orbital is hydrogen’s lowest-energy state. Its pale yellow cloud is most densely packed near the nucleus and thins smoothly with distance. Rotate it from several directions: if no viewpoint reveals an edge, lobe, or preferred direction, the evidence supports spherical symmetry.

Now scan the shell outward. Although the cloud is densest at the center, a shell very near the center contains little space and therefore few outcomes. Larger shells include more space. Their competition with the thinning cloud produces a radial-probability peak near one Bohr radius, a0. Continue outward past the peak: the fading tail has no hard boundary, even when only a few sample points remain visible.

Simulation 02

The 2s Orbital

The excited 2s state remains spherical, but its outcomes spread through a much larger volume. A compact inner region and a broad outer region are separated by a radial node at 2a0: an entire spherical surface where the probability of measuring the electron is zero.

A flat image can make this empty region look like a ring. Rotate the cloud and watch the gap remain wrapped around the nucleus; that motion reveals it as a sphere. Then scan from the inner peak, through the zero at the node, and into the larger outer peak. Compare the green 2s graph with the faint yellow 1s reference to explain how excitation changes both the size and internal structure of the probability distribution. Turn on 90% Region to see one nucleus-centered sphere containing 90% probability. It surrounds both clusters without removing the radial node inside it.

Simulation 03

The 2p Orbitals

In ideal hydrogen, 2s and 2p belong to the same principal energy level, yet their spatial patterns differ. A 2p cloud points along one axis and contains two lobes. Between them is an angular node: a whole plane through the nucleus where the probability is zero.

Switch among 2px, 2py, and 2pz with the viewer buttons or XR palm menu. The subscript names the axis along which the lobes point, so the nodal plane is perpendicular to that axis. Rotate the cloud and labeled axes together to inspect this relationship from another angle. Now grow the probability enclosure. Both lobes together contain the displayed percentage. Dense space enters first; capturing the next portion of probability eventually requires much more volume. The graph plots enclosed volume vertically against probability horizontally. A steeper curve means that capturing the next portion of probability requires more space. Its 50% and 90% guides let you read how much space a chosen probability needs. Changing orientation rotates the lobes but leaves this relationship unchanged.

Hold coverage fixed and Resample. The model predicts the fraction of outcomes inside the enclosure, not an exact count for each set. Watch the yellow measured fractions settle toward the model as independent outcomes accumulate.

Simulation 04

The 3d Orbitals

The five real 3d orbitals reveal how direction can organize a probability cloud. Select 3dxy, 3dxz, and 3dyz: four lobes lie between the named axes. In 3dx²−y², the lobes instead point along X and Y. Rotate the cloud and its axes together; a viewpoint that overlaps two lobes can conceal a structure that another view makes clear. These four clover-shaped states have two nodal planes and differ by orientation. All 3d states have no radial nodes.

Select 3dz². In the elevated desktop view, or by moving your viewpoint in XR, look for two axial lobes and an equatorial ring. Rotate about Z: unlike a clover-shaped enclosure, this enclosure looks unchanged because it is symmetric around that axis. The two angular nodes are cones separating that ring from the polar lobes, rather than flat planes. These are all hydrogen states; real atoms with several electrons need a more elaborate model. The 3d viewer uses a smaller display scale to keep this more extensive cloud in view; graph volumes remain in physical units.

Lower coverage below 30%, then increase it slowly. The densest polar regions enter first. Near 29.8% the equatorial ring begins to enter the enclosure: find the corresponding graph marker. The ring was already part of the orbital; changing coverage reveals which parts meet the selected density threshold. Continue toward 90% and read the extra volume needed for each increase in probability. Resample at a fixed coverage: geometry stays steady while the highlighted measurement count changes. Follow the relationship between space, probability, and outcomes.
